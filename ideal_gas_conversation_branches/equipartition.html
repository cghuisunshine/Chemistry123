Why is the average energy 3kBT/2?

Follow the statistical argument behind the energy–temperature relation.

What needs to be derived?

We want the average kinetic energy of a single classical gas molecule in thermal equilibrium, not just its instantaneous energy.

To calculate an average, first we must know how likely each molecular microstate is.

1. Start with the molecule and its thermal reservoir

The combined system has fixed total energy. If the molecule occupies a particular microstate s with energy εs, the surrounding reservoir must have energy Etotal − εs.

2. Turn the reservoir count into a probability weight

Boltzmann entropy relates the number of available states Ω to entropy S, while equilibrium thermodynamics relates the entropy derivative to temperature.

For a very large reservoir, taking a small energy ε from it decreases its entropy approximately by ε/T.

Exponentiating the entropy gives the familiar Boltzmann factor: a particular higher-energy microstate receives a smaller statistical weight at positive temperature.

That factor determines the classical equilibrium distribution of molecular velocity.

The logical distinction

This derivation relies on classical equilibrium statistics. Newton’s laws alone give the momentum transfer, but do not establish the equilibrium probability distribution.

Why is a velocity component Gaussian?

A quadratic kinetic energy inside the Boltzmann factor produces the Gaussian shape.

1. State the physical assumptions

Consider a classical, nonrelativistic gas at thermal equilibrium with T > 0, in the gas’s rest frame. The translational kinetic energy of a molecule of mass m is

E = (m/2)(vx² + vy² + vz²)

Classical momentum states are counted with a uniform phase-space measure. Changing from momentum to velocity gives d³p = m³ d³v; the constant factor m³ changes only normalization.

What does the Boltzmann weight mean? →

2. Substitute kinetic energy into the Boltzmann factor

F(vx, vy, vz) = A exp(−E/(kBT)) a ≡ m/(2kBT) F = A exp(−a(vx² + vy² + vz²)) = A exp(−avx²) exp(−avy²) exp(−avz²)

a is just shorthand. Its units are inverse velocity squared, so avₓ² is dimensionless. The exponential factorizes because the kinetic energy is a sum of three separate squared components.

3. Sum over the unobserved velocity components

To ask only about vₓ, allow every possible vᵧ and vz. Summing a continuous range means integrating:

f(vx) = ∫−∞∞ ∫−∞∞ F(vx,vy,vz) dvy dvz = A exp(−avx²) × ∫−∞∞ exp(−avy²) dvy × ∫−∞∞ exp(−avz²) dvz = C exp(−avx²)

Both integrals are constants with respect to vₓ. Absorb them and A into C. An exponential of a negative squared variable is the Gaussian form. Its shape has been derived from the equilibrium weight and the kinetic-energy formula.

4. Determine C from total probability one

1 = ∫−∞∞ f(vx) dvx = C ∫−∞∞ exp(−avx²) dvx = C √(π/a) C = √(a/π) = √(m/(2πkBT))

C fixes the overall scale; a sets the width. Higher temperature makes a smaller and broadens the distribution. At a given temperature, a larger molecular mass narrows it.

5. Interpret the density and recover equipartition

f(vₓ) is a probability density, with units inverse velocity. The probability for a finite interval [u₁,u₂] is ∫ from u₁ to u₂ of f(vₓ) dvₓ. For a tiny interval of width δv, this is approximately f(vₓ)δv.

⟨vx⟩ = 0 ⟨vx²⟩ = 1/(2a) = kBT/m ⟨mvx²/2⟩ = kBT/2

Opposite components ±vₓ have the same energy, so the curve is symmetric about zero. The variance follows by differentiating the Gaussian integral with respect to a, or by the integration-by-parts argument on the parent page. The three-dimensional speed distribution has an additional state-counting factor because it groups many directions together.

Is the height f(0) the probability of exactly vₓ = 0?

For a continuous distribution, the probability of one exact point is zero. A finite interval has a probability given by the area under the density curve, not its height alone.

Is the temperature relation a definition or a derivation?

Separate the definition of thermodynamic temperature, an equivalent thermodynamic identity, and the later approximation.

1. Identify this particular equation

SR is reservoir entropy, ER is reservoir energy, and the subscript V,N in the original equation means that reservoir volume and particle number stay fixed while energy changes. We write these constraints explicitly as VR,NR below. R labels the reservoir; the relation also applies to other equilibrium systems with the stated constraints.

In an entropy-based statistical mechanics treatment, this relation defines thermodynamic temperature: temperature is the reciprocal of the entropy–energy slope. If temperature and entropy have already been introduced thermodynamically, the same equation follows as an identity.

2. Derive the identity from thermodynamics

For equilibrium states of a simple compressible system with one particle species, start with the fundamental thermodynamic relation:

dER = T dSR − p dVR + μ dNR
Unpack the starting relation

Combine energy conservation with reversible heat and volume work at fixed particle number, then define the coefficient describing a change in particle number.

Here VR and NR are the reservoir volume and particle number; T, p, and μ are its temperature, pressure, and chemical potential. At fixed VR and NR, dVR = dNR = 0. Hence

dER = T dSR dSR = (1/T) dER (∂SR/∂ER)Vᵣ,Nᵣ = 1/T

The coefficient of dER is the required partial derivative. Equivalently, along a reversible path at fixed VR,NR with only pressure–volume work, dSR = δQrev/T and δQrev = dER.

3. Read the slope physically

For a sufficiently small energy change under these constraints, ΔSR ≈ ΔER/T. At 300 K, a 3 J increase corresponds to approximately 0.01 J K−1 of entropy, provided the temperature change is negligible.

For a finite change at fixed reservoir volume and particle number, ΔSR = ∫ dER/T. Keep T inside the integral if it varies. The local differential does not imply ER = TSR. See the finite-change example and product-rule explanation →

At thermal equilibrium, total entropy is stationary with respect to energy transferred between two subsystems. Their entropy–energy slopes match; therefore their temperatures match. This helps explain why this derivative describes thermal contact.

4. The approximation occurs in the Taylor expansion

Expand reservoir entropy around an energy E0, at fixed VR,NR. The first-order term uses the derivative evaluated at that state:

SR(E0 − ε) ≈ SR(E0) − ε(∂SR/∂ER)E₀,Vᵣ,Nᵣ = SR(E0) − ε/T

The temperature relation is a thermodynamic identity. Dropping the quadratic and higher Taylor terms is the approximation. A large reservoir makes its temperature change negligible over the relevant energy exchanges.

SR = kB ln ΩR (∂ ln ΩR/∂ER)Vᵣ,Nᵣ = 1/(kBT) ≡ β pi = e−βεᵢ/Z

S has units J K−1, so ∂S/∂E has units K−1. By contrast, β has units J−1; the factor kB is essential.

5. Keep the starting points separate

Three different roles

The reservoir probability formula is derived by counting. The entropy–energy derivative defines temperature in one approach and follows from established thermodynamics in another. The Boltzmann reservoir calculation then makes a first-order approximation.

Why total energy minus epsilon?

Energy conservation fixes which reservoir states can accompany one chosen system microstate.

1. The argument belongs to the reservoir

The symbol ΩR(E) is defined to count reservoir microstates at reservoir energy E. A system microstate i has energy εi. If the system and reservoir exchange energy while their total stays fixed, and their interaction energy is negligible,

Etotal = εi + ER ER = Etotal − εi

For example, with 10 energy units in total and 3 in the small system, the reservoir has 7. Its count is ΩR(7), evaluated at the energy it actually has. ΩR(3) would count the reservoir at a different energy.

2. The probability formula is derived

The equilibrium postulate assigns equal probability to every accessible microstate of the isolated combined system. Each combined state specifies both the small system and the reservoir. Fixing i leaves ΩR(Etotal − εi) compatible reservoir states. Summing their equal probabilities gives

pi = ΩR(Etotal − εi) / Ωtotal(Etotal) Ωtotal(Etotal) = ∑j ΩR(Etotal − εj)

The denominator is the same for every i. Therefore pi ∝ ΩR(Etotal − εi). This is a derived result, conditional on the postulate and the energy constraint. It still depends on εi, through the energy left for the reservoir.

3. Count four complete states

Take four distinguishable two-level units, with energies 0 and Δ. Unit S is the small system; A, B, and C form the reservoir. Fix the total energy at one quantum, Δ. The accessible combined states differ by which unit holds that quantum.

All four combined microstates are equally likely.
Excited unitSystem energyReservoir energy
SΔ0
A0Δ
B0Δ
C0Δ
P(ES = 0) = 3/4 P(ES = Δ) = 1/4

This is exact counting in a small example. A reservoir large enough to maintain a constant temperature is a further assumption used for the Boltzmann approximation.

4. A microstate and an energy level differ

The formula above fixes one system microstate. If gS(ε) different system microstates share an energy ε, add their probabilities:

P(ES = ε) ∝ gS(ε) ΩR(Etotal − ε)

For a large thermal reservoir, this becomes P(ES = ε) ∝ gS(ε)e−ε/(kBT). The degeneracy factor belongs to an energy-level probability. For continuous energy, use a density of states and an energy interval.

5. Continue to entropy and temperature

To turn the reservoir count into an exponential weight, use SR = kB ln ΩR and expand the entropy of a large reservoir. The slope used in that expansion is 1/T.

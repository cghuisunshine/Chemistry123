The ideal gas law

A gas has an observable pressure, volume, amount, and temperature. The ideal gas law connects them.

PPressurePa or kPa
VVolumem³ or L
nAmountmol
TAbsolute temperatureK

Here R is the ideal gas constant. When pressure is measured in kPa and volume in L, R = 8.314 L·kPa·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹.

What is happening at the molecular level?

Think about a closed box containing gas. Its molecules constantly move in different directions. They strike the walls, bounce back, and change momentum. The walls receive the opposite momentum: that is the mechanical origin of gas pressure.

Temperature also has a microscopic interpretation. For a classical ideal gas at thermal equilibrium, the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule is 3kBT/2. There are three independent directions of motion, and each contributes equally on average.

Putting molecular momentum transfer together with the energy–temperature relation produces the equation we started with.

What is assumed?

The result applies to an idealized gas: molecules are very small compared with their separation, interactions outside collisions are negligible, and the gas is in classical thermal equilibrium. Real gases approach this behavior under suitable conditions.

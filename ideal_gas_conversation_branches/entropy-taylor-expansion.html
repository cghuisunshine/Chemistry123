How is the reservoir entropy expanded?

Apply the Taylor formula with a negative energy change, then identify the entropy slope with reciprocal temperature.

1. Start with the ordinary Taylor formula

For a smooth function f near a reference point a, Taylor expansion writes its nearby value in terms of its value and derivatives at a:

f(a + h) = f(a) + h f′(a) + (h²/2) f″(a) + …

The first-order approximation keeps only the constant and linear terms. The derivatives are evaluated at the reference point a, not at the shifted point a + h.

2. Substitute the reservoir variables

Hold volume V and particle number N fixed. Entropy is then a function of the reservoir energy alone for this calculation. The small system gains energy εs, so the reservoir loses that amount:

f(ER) = SR(ER, V, N) a = Etotal h = −εs S₀ ≡ SR(Etotal, V, N)

S₀ is the name given to the reservoir entropy at the chosen reference energy. It is a reference value for the reservoir, held fixed while comparing different small-system microstates.

3. Keep track of the signs and the evaluation point

SR(Etotal − εs) = S₀ − εs SR′(Etotal) + (εs²/2) SR″(Etotal) + …

The linear term is negative because h = −εs. In the quadratic term, h² = εs², so the coefficient has a plus sign; the value of the second derivative may still be negative. Primes here mean energy derivatives with V,N fixed.

SR(Etotal − εs) ≈ S₀ − εs(∂SR/∂ER)V,N evaluated at ER = Etotal

4. Use the temperature identity

At the reference state, the entropy–energy slope is 1/T. Consequently,

SR(Etotal − εs) ≈ S₀ − εs/T

This is the expression used in the Boltzmann derivation. Its approximation is the removal of higher Taylor terms. A sufficiently large thermal reservoir has nearly unchanged temperature during the relevant exchanges, making the local linear approximation useful.

What if the reservoir gains a small positive energy δE instead?

Use h = +δE. The first-order expression is S_R(E_total + δE) ≈ S₀ + δE/T, with T evaluated at the reference state.

5. Convert entropy into the Boltzmann weight

ΩR = exp(SR/kB) ΩR(Etotal − εs) ≈ exp(S₀/kB) exp(−εs/(kBT))

The first factor is the same for every small-system state. It cancels in normalized probabilities. The remaining factor is the state’s Boltzmann weight.

Why does dE = T dS − p dV + μ dN?

Identify the physical laws and definitions behind each coefficient before using the temperature identity.

1. Start with energy conservation at fixed particle number

Take a simple compressible system with one particle species. The subscript R labels the reservoir: ER is its internal energy, SR its entropy, VR its volume, and NR its particle number. T, p, and μ denote its temperature, pressure, and chemical potential. First keep NR fixed. Count heat into the reservoir as positive and work on it as positive:

dER = δQ + δWon

This is the first law. Heat and work describe energy crossing the boundary. The symbols δQ and δW distinguish these path-dependent transfers from changes in the state function ER.

2. Identify the reversible heat term

For a reversible comparison of nearby equilibrium states, the thermodynamic entropy relation gives

dSR = δQrev/T δQrev = T dSR
Entropy and the second law

The integral of δQrev/T around a reversible cycle is zero. Its integral between equilibrium endpoints therefore defines an entropy difference.

This uses entropy and thermodynamic temperature, whose relation is justified by the second law and reversible cycles. Energy conservation alone does not identify this entropy term. T is measured in kelvin and S in J K−1, so T dS has units of energy.

3. Identify the reversible volume-work term

A gas expanding against an external load performs work on its surroundings. For a frictionless piston with negligible energy storage, the work on the gas is −pext dVR. In the reversible mechanical limit, pext approaches the equilibrium gas pressure p through infinitesimal differences:

δWon,rev = −p dVR dER = T dSR − p dVR (fixed NR)
Force times displacement

The external force points inward while an expanding piston moves outward. With dV = A dx, work on the gas is −pext dV; the reversible limit replaces pext by p.

↳ Follow-up questionWhy is pressure–volume work −p dV?Track a piston displacement and the direction of the external load before taking the reversible limit.↗

Expansion has dVR > 0 and therefore negative work on the system. Compression has dVR < 0 and positive work on it. The simple compressible model includes volume work; other work modes would require additional terms.

4. Allow particle number to change

Equilibrium states of this model can be represented by ER(SR,VR,NR). Its total differential has one coefficient for each variable:

dER = (∂ER/∂SR)Vᵣ,Nᵣ dSR + (∂ER/∂VR)Sᵣ,Nᵣ dVR + (∂ER/∂NR)Sᵣ,Vᵣ dNR

Comparing this differential with the fixed-particle-number result identifies the first two coefficients as T and −p. Define the third coefficient as the chemical potential μ:

T = (∂ER/∂SR)Vᵣ,Nᵣ −p = (∂ER/∂VR)Sᵣ,Nᵣ μ ≡ (∂ER/∂NR)Sᵣ,Vᵣ dER = T dSR − p dVR + μ dNR

μ describes the equilibrium internal-energy change per added particle while total entropy and volume stay fixed. It is not generally the mean energy of a particle or the energy delivered by an actual incoming stream. An open-system transport balance needs its own material-flow terms. With NR counting particles, μ has units J per particle; p has units Pa and VR has units m³, so p dVR is in joules. If mole number n replaces particle number NR, use molar chemical potential in J/mol in μ dn.

5. Return to the temperature derivative

For nonzero temperature, divide the fundamental relation by T and solve for the entropy differential:

dSR = (1/T) dER + (p/T) dVR − (μ/T) dNR

Holding both VR and NR fixed makes dVR = dNR = 0:

dER = T dSR dSR = (1/T) dER (∂SR/∂ER)Vᵣ,Nᵣ = 1/T

The coefficient 1/T is the entropy gained per small energy increase under these constraints. Its units are (J/K)/J = K−1. At 300 K, a small 3 J energy increase gives approximately 3/300 = 0.010 J/K of entropy. At a higher positive temperature, the same small energy increase produces a smaller entropy increase.

At fixed reservoir volume and particle number, a small reversible heat input is 60 J at 300 K. What changes?

Volume work is zero. The internal-energy increase is 60 J and the entropy increase is approximately 60/300 = 0.20 J/K, provided the temperature change is negligible.

6. Distinguish differentials from finite changes

The temperature in dSR = dER/T is the local equilibrium temperature. At fixed VR and NR, integrate between equilibrium states A and B:

ΔSR = ∫Eᵣ,AEᵣ,B dER / T(ER,VR,NR)

If T stays constant along that comparison, this becomes ΔSR = ΔER/T. For a sufficiently small change, using the starting temperature gives a local approximation. If T changes appreciably, keep it inside the integral.

For example, a body with constant heat capacity CV at fixed volume and particle number has dER = CV dT:

ΔER = CV(TB − TA) ΔSR = CV ln(TB/TA)

With CV = 10 J/K and heating from 300 K to 600 K, ΔER = 3000 J while ΔSR = 10 ln 2 ≈ 6.93 J/K. Dividing by the starting temperature would give 10 J/K and would be incorrect. Such a finite body does not behave as a constant-temperature reservoir over this change.

The differential relation also does not imply ER = TSR. The product rule gives d(TSR) = T dSR + SR dT, and integration requires a reference value. Even when T is constant along a comparison, only the changes obey ΔER = TΔSR.

7. Keep the state identity separate from actual transfers

Reversible heat and work identify the coefficients using a comparison of neighboring equilibrium states. The resulting fundamental relation is a state identity. It does not assert that an actual process between those states is reversible.

During an actual irreversible process, heat need not equal T dS and boundary work depends on external pressure. Evaluate actual transfers with the first law and the process conditions; evaluate entropy differences from the equilibrium endpoints using a suitable reversible comparison.

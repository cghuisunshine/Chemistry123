What does Boltzmann weight mean?

Compare microscopic states using relative weights, then normalize them into probabilities.

1. A weight is a relative probability score

A particular system microstate i has energy Ei. Its Boltzmann weight is a dimensionless number:

wi ≡ exp(−Ei/(kBT)) Z ≡ ∑j wj pi = wi/Z

The word weight here refers to relative likelihood. The weights do not have to sum to one; probabilities do. Z is the partition function, the sum of all microstate weights.

pi/pj = wi/wj = exp(−(Ei − Ej)/(kBT))

2. Count the reservoir states that support each system state

Assume weak coupling so energies add, equilibrium equal probabilities for the isolated combined system, and a large thermal reservoir. Fixing microstate i leaves reservoir energy Etotal − Ei. Counting compatible combined states gives

pi ∝ ΩR(Etotal − Ei)

For an ordinary positive-temperature reservoir, giving more energy to the small system leaves fewer compatible reservoir states. That is the physical origin of the preference for individual lower-energy microstates.

3. Derive the exponential weight

SR = kB ln ΩR SR(Etotal − Ei) ≈ S₀ − Ei/T ΩR(Etotal − Ei) ≈ exp(S₀/kB) exp(−Ei/(kBT))

The factor exp(S₀/kB) is shared by all states. It cancels on division by the sum of weights. The remaining state-dependent factor is exp(−Ei/(kBT)). The physical assumptions entered in the counting argument and the large-reservoir approximation.

4. Normalize a two-state example

Suppose there are exactly two nondegenerate states with energies 0 and kBT. At that temperature,

w₀ = 1, w₁ = exp(−1) ≈ 0.368 Z = 1 + exp(−1) p₀ ≈ 0.731, p₁ ≈ 0.269

The higher-energy state is about 36.8% as likely as the lower-energy state; its normalized probability is about 26.9%. A probability ratio and a probability are different quantities.

Does adding the same constant energy E* to every state change these probabilities?

Every weight is multiplied by the same factor exp(−E*/(k_B T)). That factor cancels against the same factor in Z, so the probabilities are unchanged.

5. Use the correct state-counting measure

If g states share the same energy E, their total energy-level weight is g exp(−E/(kBT)). For continuous variables, the weight enters a density and probability is obtained by integration. An energy density also needs a density-of-states factor.

In Cartesian momentum space the classical phase-space measure is uniform. For fixed mass, p = mv has a constant Jacobian, so equal infinitesimal Cartesian velocity volumes acquire the same measure factor. Putting the quadratic kinetic energy into the Boltzmann weight therefore gives the Gaussian velocity density.

Pressure from molecular collisions

A Newtonian derivation, from one wall collision to the pressure of a gas.

Start with one molecule in a cube

Imagine a cubic container with side length L. A molecule of mass m approaches the right wall with velocity component vx. Elastic reflection reverses the x-component of velocity.

distance between walls = L · x-motion only

1. Momentum transferred in one collision

The molecule starts with momentum mvx and rebounds with −mvx. Its change is −2mvx, so the wall receives an impulse of +2mvx.

2. Time between impacts on the same wall

Between two successive impacts on the right wall, the molecule travels a distance 2L in the x-direction.

3. Average force

Average force is impulse divided by elapsed time. The sign of velocity no longer matters because the force depends on the square of the component.

From force to pressure

The wall has area L², and the cube has volume V = L³. Divide the force by area to obtain one molecule’s contribution, then add the contributions from N molecules.

In equilibrium, no spatial direction is preferred, so ⟨vx²⟩ = ⟨vy²⟩ = ⟨vz²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩/3.

We have now derived pressure from mechanical collisions, without yet making any assumption about the numerical relationship between temperature and molecular kinetic energy.

Important boundary

The wall-collision calculation uses Newtonian mechanics. The energy–temperature relation also needs equilibrium statistical mechanics. That is the new idea in the branch above.

Start with the equation [A]ₜ = [A]₀ exp(−kt)

First-order decay and half-life

Integrate a first-order disappearance law, then derive its half-life.

The equation and what it means

[A]ₜ = [A]₀ exp(−kt) t½ = ln 2/k

Integrate a first-order disappearance law, then derive its half-life.

Symbols and units. [A]: concentration (mol L⁻¹); k: inverse time (s⁻¹ if t is in seconds); t and t½: time. The logarithm uses [A]ₜ/[A]₀, a dimensionless ratio.

What can we start from?

  • Empirical law. Assume the measured disappearance law is −d[A]/dt = k[A].
  • Calculus. For x > 0, ∫ dx/x gives a logarithmic ratio between limits.
  • Definition. Half-life is the time when [A]ₜ = [A]₀/2.
Prerequisites

We assume basic university mathematics and physics: differentiation, integration, vectors, energy conservation, and elementary probability. The chemical definitions and thermodynamic premises are stated explicitly; an empirical rate law or model is not proved by rearranging it.

1. Separate the variables

Move every concentration factor to the left and time to the right.

d[A]/[A] = −k dt

The negative sign ensures the concentration decreases for k > 0.

2. Integrate with limits

Integrate from [A]₀ to [A]ₜ and from 0 to t.

ln([A]ₜ/[A]₀) = −kt

The ratio inside ln is dimensionless, and kt is also dimensionless.

3. Solve for concentration

Exponentiate both sides and isolate [A]ₜ.

[A]ₜ = [A]₀ exp(−kt)

The concentration drops by the same fraction in each equal time interval.

↳ Follow-up: What does a straight logarithmic plot establish? How do data test a first-order model?Follow the argument, then return to this exact statement. ↗

4. Derive the half-life

Set [A]ₜ/[A]₀ = 1/2 and solve for t.

−ln 2 = −kt½ t½ = ln 2/k

The initial concentration cancels, so first-order half-life does not depend on [A]₀.

Conditions and limits

  • The disappearance law is first order in A over the interval.
  • k is constant and volume is fixed, with no competing source of A.

The kinetic law is an explicit experimental/model premise. Energy conservation and stoichiometry alone do not determine it.

Common misconception

A reaction is first order whenever its balanced equation has one A.

Overall reaction order is measured or justified by a mechanism. It is not inferred from an overall balanced equation.

Check the result

A straight line of ln([A]ₜ/[A]₀) versus t has slope −k. A concentration-versus-time line would not be straight for first-order decay.

If k = 0.100 s⁻¹, what fraction of A remains after 13.86 s?

t½ = 0.693/0.100 = 6.93 s. After two half-lives, (1/2)² = 1/4 remains.

Why Qh/Th = Qc/Tc

Start with the Carnot cycle. Open a “why” exactly where you need a deeper explanation.

The relation and its assumptions

Qh/Th = Qc/Tc

For a reversible Carnot engine, the heat absorbed from the hot reservoir divided by its temperature equals the heat released to the cold reservoir divided by its temperature.

Symbols. Qh, Qc: positive heat amounts (J); Th > Tc: absolute temperatures (K); V₁…V₄: gas volumes (m³); n: amount (mol); R: 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Each heat–temperature ratio has units J K⁻¹.

This proof uses a fixed amount of ideal gas, reversible processes, and only pressure–volume work. We assume constant molar heat capacities when using the power law for the adiabatic stages. The Carnot relation itself is more general than that model.

Sign convention

Work W done by the gas is positive: dU = δQ − δW. In chemistry notation, work w done on the gas is positive: dU = δQ + δw. These express the same energy balance.

The four stages

StageProcessEnergy transfer
1 → 2Isothermal expansion at ThAbsorbs Qh
2 → 3Adiabatic expansion to TcNo heat; gas cools
3 → 4Isothermal compression at TcReleases Qc
4 → 1Adiabatic compression to ThNo heat; gas warms

1. Heat absorbed at the hot temperature

An ideal gas’s internal energy is unchanged during an isothermal process. The first law therefore gives Qh = W₁₂. For reversible piston work, δW = P dV; substituting P = nRTh/V gives:

Qh = ∫V₁V₂ P dV = nRTh ∫V₁V₂ dV/V = nRTh ln(V₂/V₁) Qh/Th = nR ln(V₂/V₁)

The logarithm comes from integrating 1/V. Dividing by temperature leaves nR times a logarithmic volume ratio.

2. Heat released at the cold temperature

During compression from V₃ to V₄, V₄ < V₃. The signed heat entering the gas is nRTc ln(V₄/V₃), which is negative. Qc denotes the positive amount released:

Qc = nRTc ln(V₃/V₄) Qc/Tc = nR ln(V₃/V₄)

We now need to prove that V₂/V₁ = V₃/V₄. The two adiabatic stages provide that connection.

3. Why are the volume ratios equal?

For a reversible adiabatic ideal-gas process with constant heat capacities:

TVγ−1 = constant, γ = Cp,m/Cv,m
Why does TVγ−1 stay constant?

Adiabatic means δQ = 0. For reversible expansion, the first law becomes:

dU = −P dV nCv,m dT = −P dV

Expansion work comes from internal energy, so the gas cools. Here Cv,m is the molar constant-volume heat capacity, measured in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Why is dU = nCv,m dT, even when volume changes?

At constant volume, with only pressure–volume work, dV = 0 means no work is done. Thus dU = δQ. The molar heat-capacity definition gives:

Cv,m = (1/n)(∂U/∂T)V δQV = nCv,m dT

For a fixed amount and composition of ideal gas, U = U(T). Consequently, the same temperature change produces the same internal-energy change along any path:

dU = nCv,m(T) dT

The subscript v identifies how the heat capacity is defined; it does not restrict this ideal-gas energy equation to constant-volume paths. Constant heat capacity is needed only to replace the integral by nCv,mΔT for a finite change.

Is U = U(T) another statement of the first law?

No. It is an additional property of the ideal-gas model, used together with the first law.

StatementWhat it says
dU = δQ − δWEnergy conservation for the closed system: heat and work change internal energy.
U = U(T)At fixed amount and composition, ideal-gas internal energy depends only on temperature.

In the microscopic ideal-gas model, particles have no interaction potential energy between them. Changing their separation does not change that energy. Their equilibrium microscopic energy depends on temperature; molecules may also store rotational and vibrational energy.

For a classical monatomic ideal gas:

U = N(3kBT/2) = 3nRT/2 dU = (3nR/2) dT, so Cv,m = 3R/2

N is the number of atoms and kB is Boltzmann’s constant; NkB = nR. The energy formula uses the classical equipartition result.

For real gases, intermolecular interactions can make U depend on both T and V. The first law still holds, but generally:

dU = nCv,m dT + (∂U/∂V)T dV

Energy conservation alone does not make the second term vanish.

Now substitute P = nRT/V into the adiabatic energy balance, cancel n, and separate the variables:

Cv,m dT = −RT dV/V dT/T = −(R/Cv,m) dV/V

For an ideal gas, Cp,m − Cv,m = R, so R/Cv,m = γ − 1. Follow the heat-capacity difference proof →

For constant heat capacities, integrate between two states:

ln(Tb/Ta) = −(γ − 1) ln(Vb/Va) TbVbγ−1 = TaVaγ−1

That is exactly what “TVγ−1 = constant” means. It applies to reversible adiabatic paths, not to every process with no heat transfer.

Apply the relation separately to expansion 2 → 3 and compression 4 → 1:

ThV₂γ−1 = TcV₃γ−1 ThV₁γ−1 = TcV₄γ−1

Divide the first equation by the second. The temperatures cancel:

(V₂/V₁)γ−1 = (V₃/V₄)γ−1 V₂/V₁ = V₃/V₄

The constants on the two separate adiabats need not be equal; we never assume that they are.

4. Substitute the equal volume ratios

Qh/Th = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = nR ln(V₃/V₄) = Qc/Tc

The temperatures divide out of the isothermal heat equations, and the adiabatic stages make the remaining logarithms equal.

For a full cycle ΔU = 0, so Wnet = Qh − Qc. This also gives the reversible Carnot efficiency:

η = Wnet/Qh = 1 − Tc/Th

Check with numbers

Let n = 1.00 mol, Th = 600 K, Tc = 300 K, and V₂/V₁ = V₃/V₄ = 2.

Qh = (1)(8.314)(600) ln 2 ≈ 3458 J Qc = (1)(8.314)(300) ln 2 ≈ 1729 J Qh/Th = Qc/Tc ≈ 5.76 J K⁻¹
If the expansion is adiabatic, must its temperature fall?

For the reversible ideal-gas expansion used here, yes: it does positive work using internal energy. But in ideal-gas free expansion into a vacuum, Q = 0 and W = 0, so ΔU = 0 and temperature stays constant. “Adiabatic” alone does not imply TVγ−1 = constant.

Why does external pressure determine work?

Which force actually crosses the boundary?

Choose a boundary and a sign

Take the gas as the system and let x increase when its piston moves outward. The external load exerts an inward force on that boundary. With a frictionless piston whose kinetic-energy change is negligible, the energy delivered to the gas as work is the load force dotted with the displacement.

δw = (−PextA) dx = −Pext dV

The symbol δ marks a path-dependent energy transfer. There is no state variable w whose endpoint difference gives every possible work transfer. The piston assumptions matter: a piston gaining kinetic energy can temporarily store energy that has not gone into the external load.

Why not just use the gas pressure?

A gas can expand into vacuum while maintaining a nonzero pressure internally. The load then receives no mechanical energy because Pext = 0. Substituting gas pressure would predict work that was never delivered. During a rapid process the gas may not even have a single uniform pressure.

For slow, mechanically reversible motion, the two pressures approach equality. Only in that limit may the equilibrium equation of state supply P in the work integral.

A finite-load calculation

Pext = 100 kPa, ΔV = 2.0 L w = −(100 kPa)(2.0 L) = −200 J

The conversion follows directly from 1 kPa·L = 1 J. Compression reverses the sign because ΔV becomes negative. No ideal-gas assumption entered the calculation.

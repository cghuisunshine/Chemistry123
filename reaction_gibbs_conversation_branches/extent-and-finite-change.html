Why is reaction Gibbs energy a derivative?

Is ΔᵣG the energy change of the whole reaction?

Parameterize composition with one coordinate

For A ⇌ 2B, define a reaction extent ξ in moles of reaction. Then nA = nA,0 − ξ and nB = nB,0 + 2ξ. This is a stoichiometric coordinate, not a new conservation law.

dG = μA(−dξ) + μB(2dξ) ΔᵣG = dG/dξ = 2μB − μA

Follow the slope as composition changes

At fixed T and P the chemical potentials change as the mixture reacts. Consequently the slope ΔᵣG is not generally constant. The total Gibbs change over a finite reaction interval is:

G(ξ₂) − G(ξ₁) = ∫ξ₁ξ₂ ΔᵣG(ξ) dξ

Replacing this integral by ΔᵣG at the initial state times the full extent would freeze the composition dependence incorrectly.

Use the sign locally

ΔᵣG < 0: forward infinitesimal change lowers G ΔᵣG > 0: reverse infinitesimal change lowers G

At an interior equilibrium the slope is zero. The standard slope ΔᵣG° need not be zero because the actual mixture generally has Q different from 1.

Why does an ideal mixture use partial pressure?

Can we derive the mixing contribution?

Start from an extensive mixture free energy

For ideal gases at a common T and total pressure P, the Gibbs energy can be written as the pure-gas reference contribution plus a mixing term. Let n = Σnᵢ and xᵢ = nᵢ/n.

G = Σ nᵢμᵢ*(T,P) + RT Σ nᵢ ln xᵢ

The mixing term corresponds to ΔSmix = −RΣnᵢ ln xᵢ and zero ideal-gas enthalpy of mixing.

Differentiate the whole mixing term

Σ nᵢ ln xᵢ = Σ nᵢ ln nᵢ − n ln n ∂/∂nⱼ = ln nⱼ + 1 − ln n − 1 = ln xⱼ

Thus μⱼ = μⱼ*(T,P) + RT ln xⱼ. Combining this with the pure ideal-gas pressure term RT ln(P/p°) gives μⱼ = μⱼ° + RT ln(xⱼP/p°). Dalton’s law identifies xⱼP = pⱼ.

State what was assumed

This proof assumes ideal mixing and a classical ideal-gas equation of state. Stoichiometry alone cannot determine activities in a real solution. Nonideal interactions modify the chemical potential through activity coefficients or fugacities, while ΔᵣG = Σνᵢμᵢ still holds.

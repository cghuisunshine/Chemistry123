Why can an extremely dilute acid not give alkaline pH?

Retain charge balance exactly within the ideal model

Let h = [H⁺], κ = Kₐc°, and W = Kw(c°)². Water gives [OH⁻] = W/h. Acid equilibrium and mass balance give [A⁻] = κc₀/(h + κ).

h = κc₀/(h + κ) + W/h

Obtain a single polynomial

h³ + κh² − (κc₀ + W)h − κW = 0

The original charge-balance equation is often more convenient to solve numerically than the cubic. For h > 0 its left-minus-right derivative is 1 + κc₀/(h + κ)² + W/h², which is positive. There is therefore a unique positive physical root.

Take the infinite-dilution limit

As c₀ tends to zero, the charge balance tends to h = W/h, giving h = √W = √Kwc°. The pH approaches neutral water at the specified temperature. A pure acid solution in this model has h greater than √W, so it cannot become alkaline simply because its analytical acid concentration is small.

Neutral pH is pKw/2; the number 7 applies only when pKw is approximately 14.

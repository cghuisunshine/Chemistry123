Why does an ideal gas have U = U(T)?

Can PV = nRT justify this extra premise?

Begin with equilibrium thermodynamics

At fixed amount and composition, regard U as a function of T and V. The fundamental relation and a Maxwell relation imply an identity that is valid for any simple compressible fluid.

(∂U/∂V)T = T(∂P/∂T)V − P

This is stronger than an algebraic rearrangement of PV = nRT: it uses the thermodynamic structure of U and S as state functions.

Insert the ideal equation of state

P = nRT/V T(∂P/∂T)V − P = T(nR/V) − nRT/V = 0

Thus changing V at fixed T does not change U. Within a connected single-phase ideal-gas region, U depends only on T for the fixed sample. Rotational and vibrational energies can change the shape of U(T); they do not invalidate this conclusion for a fixed-composition ideal gas.

A microscopic interpretation

If intermolecular interaction energy is negligible, moving molecules farther apart does not change their potential energy. Equilibrium translational and internal molecular energies are set by temperature. A real gas can have an interaction-energy contribution depending on density, so its internal energy need not have the same property.

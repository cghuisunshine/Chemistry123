Vapour pressure and temperature

Reduce the exact phase-boundary relation to a useful vapour-pressure law and identify both approximations.

The equation and what it means

d ln p/dT = ΔᵥₐₚHₘ/(RT²) ln(p₂/p₁) = −(ΔᵥₐₚHₘ/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

Reduce the exact phase-boundary relation to a useful vapour-pressure law and identify both approximations.

Symbols and units. p₁,p₂: equilibrium vapour pressures in the same units; T₁,T₂: K; ΔvapHₘ: J mol⁻¹; R: J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. p₂/p₁ is dimensionless.

Textbook source. Equation 4.11 · Equation 4.12. Open the exact expression and its context.

What can we start from?

  • Phase equilibrium. Clapeyron: dp/dT = ΔᵥₐₚHₘ/[T(Vₘ,g − Vₘ,l)].
  • Model. For dilute ideal vapour, Vₘ,g = RT/p and Vₘ,l ≪ Vₘ,g.
  • Calculus. dp/p = d ln p; ∫T⁻² dT = −1/T.
Prerequisites

We assume basic university mathematics and physics: differentiation, integration, vectors, energy conservation, and elementary probability. The chemical definitions and thermodynamic premises are stated explicitly; an empirical rate law or model is not proved by rearranging it.

1. Approximate the volume change

Replace Vₘ,g − Vₘ,l using the gas model.

ΔᵥₐₚVₘ ≈ RT/p dp/dT ≈ pΔᵥₐₚHₘ/(RT²)

A mole of low-density vapour takes much more space than a mole of liquid.

2. Use the logarithmic derivative

Divide by p.

d ln p/dT = ΔᵥₐₚHₘ/(RT²)

Positive vaporization enthalpy makes vapour pressure rise with temperature.

3. Integrate between two coexistence states

Treat ΔvapHₘ as constant and integrate with limits.

ln(p₂/p₁) = −(ΔᵥₐₚHₘ/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

The pressure ratio is unit-independent; temperature must be absolute.

↳ Follow-up: Which step actually needs a constant latent heat? What if vaporization enthalpy changes with temperature?Follow the argument, then return to this exact statement. ↗

Conditions and limits

  • The liquid and vapour are in equilibrium, the vapour is approximately ideal, and liquid molar volume is negligible.
  • The molar enthalpy of vaporization is approximately constant between T₁ and T₂ for the integrated expression.

The approximation becomes poor near the critical point or at high vapour density. This is a phase-equilibrium law; the similar van ’t Hoff expression describes reaction K.

Common misconception

The differential equation also requires constant vaporization enthalpy.

The differential form can use ΔvapHₘ(T). Constant enthalpy is an additional assumption used to perform the short integral.

Check the result

For ΔvapHₘ = 40.0 kJ mol⁻¹ from 300 K to 310 K, ln(p₂/p₁) ≈ 0.517 and p₂/p₁ ≈ 1.68.

With positive ΔvapHₘ and T₂ > T₁, how do the vapour pressures compare?

1/T₂ − 1/T₁ is negative; the leading minus sign gives ln(p₂/p₁) > 0.

Temperature dependence of K

Derive the van ’t Hoff equation, including the extra assumption needed for its two-temperature form.

The equation and what it means

d ln K/dT = ΔᵣH°/(RT²) ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔᵣH°/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

Derive the van ’t Hoff equation, including the extra assumption needed for its two-temperature form.

Symbols and units. K₁, K₂: equilibrium constants at T₁, T₂ (K); ΔᵣH°: J mol⁻¹; R: J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

What can we start from?

  • Equilibrium. ΔᵣG°/T = −R ln K.
  • Definition. ΔᵣG° = ΔᵣH° − TΔᵣS°.
  • Fundamental. At fixed standard pressure, d(ΔᵣG°)/dT = −ΔᵣS°, from dG = −S dT + V dP.
Prerequisites

We assume basic university mathematics and physics: differentiation, integration, vectors, energy conservation, and elementary probability. The chemical definitions and thermodynamic premises are stated explicitly; an empirical rate law or model is not proved by rearranging it.

1. Differentiate G°/T

Use the quotient rule and d(ΔᵣG°)/dT = −ΔᵣS°.

d(ΔᵣG°/T)/dT = (−TΔᵣS° − ΔᵣG°)/T²

The derivative acts on both the numerator and 1/T.

2. Eliminate entropy

Use ΔᵣG° = ΔᵣH° − TΔᵣS° in the numerator.

d(ΔᵣG°/T)/dT = −ΔᵣH°/T²

This is the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation for the reaction at fixed standard pressure.

3. Differentiate the K relation

Differentiate ΔᵣG°/T = −R ln K and compare.

−R d ln K/dT = −ΔᵣH°/T² d ln K/dT = ΔᵣH°/(RT²)

Endothermic reactions have a positive derivative: their K increases as temperature rises.

4. Integrate with the additional approximation

Assume ΔᵣH° is constant between T₁ and T₂. Integrate both sides.

ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔᵣH°/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

State the constant-enthalpy approximation explicitly rather than treating this integrated form as exact over every interval.

Conditions and limits

  • The same reaction and standard-state convention apply at both temperatures.
  • ΔᵣH° is approximately constant over the interval for the integrated two-temperature form.

The differential equation does not require constant ΔᵣH°. If it varies appreciably, integrate ΔᵣH°(T)/(RT²) instead of taking it outside the integral.

Common misconception

ΔᵣG° is independent of temperature.

Its temperature derivative is −ΔᵣS°. Treating it as constant would discard the entropy contribution.

Check the result

With ΔᵣH° ≈ +40.0 kJ mol⁻¹ from 300 K to 310 K, ln(K₂/K₁) ≈ 0.517, so K₂/K₁ ≈ 1.68.

For an endothermic reaction with ΔᵣH° > 0, how does K respond to increasing T?

d ln K/dT = ΔᵣH°/(RT²) is positive. The conclusion concerns equilibrium, not how quickly the reaction runs.

Why does k[A][B] require another formula?

Is every second-order integrated law the same?

Use stoichiometry to keep one independent variable

For A + B → products at fixed volume, let initial concentrations be a and b and let x be the reacted concentration. Then [A] = a − x, [B] = b − x, and dx/dt = k(a − x)(b − x).

Integrate for unequal initial concentrations

1/[(a − x)(b − x)] = [1/(a − x) − 1/(b − x)]/(b − a) kt = [1/(b − a)] ln{a(b − x)/[b(a − x)]}

The logarithm compares dimensionless ratios. This formula is valid for a ≠ b while both reactant concentrations remain positive. Substituting x = 0 gives zero elapsed time.

Recover the familiar special cases

If a = b, the concentrations remain equal and the law becomes −d[A]/dt = k[A]², giving the reciprocal formula. If b is much larger than a and barely depleted, the approximate law is first order in A with kobs = kb.

Reaction order specifies the total concentration power; it does not uniquely specify the integral when multiple concentrations vary.

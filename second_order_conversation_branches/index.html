Second-order decay and half-life

Derive the integrated law for −d[A]/dt = k[A]² and explain why its half-life depends on initial concentration.

The equation and what it means

1/[A]ₜ = 1/[A]₀ + kt t½ = 1/(k[A]₀)

Derive the integrated law for −d[A]/dt = k[A]² and explain why its half-life depends on initial concentration.

Symbols and units. [A]: mol L⁻¹; k: L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ when t is in s; 1/[A]: L mol⁻¹; t½: s.

What can we start from?

  • Empirical law. Assume the disappearance law is −d[A]/dt = k[A]².
  • Calculus. ∫ x⁻² dx = −1/x + constant.
  • Definition. At t½, [A]ₜ = [A]₀/2.
Prerequisites

We assume basic university mathematics and physics: differentiation, integration, vectors, energy conservation, and elementary probability. The chemical definitions and thermodynamic premises are stated explicitly; an empirical rate law or model is not proved by rearranging it.

1. Separate concentration from time

Rearrange the differential rate law for integration.

d[A]/[A]² = −k dt

Unlike first order, this integral involves [A]⁻² rather than [A]⁻¹.

2. Integrate with endpoint limits

Evaluate both definite integrals carefully, keeping the signs.

−1/[A]ₜ + 1/[A]₀ = −kt

Substitution of limits supplies the initial-condition term.

3. Rearrange into a line

Multiply by −1 and isolate 1/[A]ₜ.

1/[A]ₜ = 1/[A]₀ + kt

A graph of 1/[A] versus t is linear with slope k.

↳ Follow-up: Is every second-order integrated law the same? Why does k[A][B] require another formula?Follow the argument, then return to this exact statement. ↗

4. Derive the half-life

Substitute [A]ₜ = [A]₀/2 and solve for t½.

2/[A]₀ − 1/[A]₀ = kt½ t½ = 1/(k[A]₀)

A larger starting concentration makes the initial disappearance faster and the half-life shorter.

Conditions and limits

  • The stated disappearance law is proportional to [A]², with constant k.
  • The volume is fixed and there is no competing source of A.

Here k is defined by disappearance of A. If a reaction rate is defined as −(1/2)d[A]/dt, a factor of two must be handled consistently.

Common misconception

This formula applies to every reaction with overall order two, including k[A][B] with different starting concentrations.

The derivation integrates one variable with a square. A two-reactant law can require a different integrated expression.

Check the result

If k = 0.50 L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ and [A]₀ = 0.20 mol L⁻¹, t½ = 10 s. The next halving takes 20 s.

For this second-order law, what happens to t½ if [A]₀ is doubled and k stays fixed?

t½ = 1/(k[A]₀); doubling the denominator halves the time.
